## Definition

Let $f : M \to N$ be smooth and $S \subseteq N$ a submanifold. $f$ is **transverse** to $S$ at $p \in f^{-1}(S)$ if:

$$df_p(T_pM) + T_{f(p)}S = T_{f(p)}N$$

i.e. the image of $df_p$ together with $T_{f(p)}S$ spans the whole tangent space at $f(p)$.

---

## Why Transversality Matters

> [!tip] Transverse preimages are manifolds
> If $f$ is transverse to $S$, then $f^{-1}(S)$ is a submanifold of $M$, with codimension equal to the codimension of $S$ in $N$. This turns intersection problems into smooth submanifold problems.

**Example**: two curves in $\mathbb{R}^2$ meeting transversally (not tangentially) intersect in isolated points — exactly the generic, stable case.

---

## Genericity

Transversality is a **generic** condition: by Sard's theorem (see [[13.3.5 Sard's theorem]]), small perturbations of $f$ can always be made transverse to $S$. This is why transversality arguments are ubiquitous — non-transverse configurations are "unstable" and can be perturbed away.

## Practice Problems

1. Show that two lines in $\mathbb{R}^2$ meeting at a single point are transverse (unless parallel or identical).
2. Give an example of a smooth map that is not transverse to a submanifold, and describe what goes wrong with the preimage.
3. Explain how transversality is used to define the intersection number of two submanifolds.
4. Verify the codimension formula for $f^{-1}(S)$ in a simple example, e.g. $f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$, $S = \{0\}$.

## Critical and Regular Values

For $f : M \to N$ smooth, $p \in M$ is a **critical point** if $df_p$ is not surjective; $q \in N$ is a **critical value** if it is $f(p)$ for some critical point $p$. Otherwise $q$ is a **regular value**.

---

## Sard's Theorem

The set of critical values of $f$ has **measure zero** in $N$. Equivalently: **almost every** value of $f$ is regular.

> [!tip] Regular values give manifolds
> If $q$ is a regular value, $f^{-1}(q)$ is a smooth submanifold of $M$ (a special case of transversality — see [[13.3.2 Transversality]] with $S = \{q\}$).

---

## Why It's Powerful

Sard's theorem guarantees that "generic" perturbations avoid pathological behavior:

- **Whitney embedding**: generic projections of a manifold into a lower-dimensional space are embeddings.
- **Transversality theorem**: small perturbations of $f$ can be made transverse to any fixed submanifold.
- **Degree theory**: the degree of a map can be computed by counting preimages of a regular value.

## Practice Problems

1. Show that if $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is $f(x) = x^2$, the only critical value is $0$, and every other value has a submanifold (two points) as preimage.
2. Explain why "almost every value is regular" is a measure-theoretic, not topological, statement.
3. Use Sard's theorem to argue that a generic smooth map $S^1 \to \mathbb{R}$ has only finitely many critical points.

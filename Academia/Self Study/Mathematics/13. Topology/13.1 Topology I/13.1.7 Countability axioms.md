## Overview

Countability axioms restrict how "large" the neighborhood structure of a space can be, using countable collections of open sets rather than arbitrary ones.

| Axiom | Name | Condition |
|-------|------|-----------|
| First countable | — | Every point has a countable neighborhood basis |
| Second countable | — | The whole space has a countable basis |
| Separable | — | There is a countable dense subset |
| Lindelöf | — | Every open cover has a countable subcover |

$$\text{second countable} \implies \text{first countable}, \ \text{separable}, \ \text{Lindelöf}$$

---

## First Countability and Sequences

First countability is exactly what makes **sequences** sufficient to describe the topology (closure, continuity, compactness can all be tested with sequences).

> [!example] Metric spaces are first countable
> The balls $B(x, 1/n)$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}$ form a countable neighborhood basis at $x$, so every metric space is first countable.

---

## Second Countability

$\mathbb{R}^n$ is second countable: open balls with rational radius and rational-coordinate center form a countable basis. Second countability is the key hypothesis in [[13.2.5 Metrization theorems]] (Urysohn's metrization theorem requires second countable + regular).

---

## Separability

A space is **separable** if it has a countable dense subset — e.g. $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$.

> [!warning] Separable does not imply second countable in general
> These coincide for metric spaces, but not for arbitrary topological spaces.

## Practice Problems

1. Show that every metric space is first countable.
2. Prove that second countability implies separability.
3. Show that $\mathbb{R}$ with the standard topology is second countable.
4. Give an example of a separable space that is not second countable.
5. Prove that a second countable space is Lindelöf.

## Overview

Separation axioms describe how well a topology can "separate" distinct points or sets using open sets. They form a hierarchy, each stronger than the last.

| Axiom | Name | Condition |
|-------|------|-----------|
| $T_0$ | Kolmogorov | For $x \neq y$, some open set contains one but not the other |
| $T_1$ | Fréchet | For $x \neq y$, each has an open set not containing the other |
| $T_2$ | Hausdorff | For $x \neq y$, disjoint open sets $U \ni x$, $V \ni y$ exist |
| $T_3$ | Regular | Points and disjoint closed sets can be separated by open sets |
| $T_4$ | Normal | Disjoint closed sets can be separated by open sets |

$$T_4 \implies T_3 \implies T_2 \implies T_1 \implies T_0$$

---

## Hausdorff Spaces

$T_2$ is by far the most used axiom in practice: it guarantees limits of sequences are unique, and that compact subsets are closed.

> [!example] Why Hausdorff matters
> All metric spaces are Hausdorff. Non-Hausdorff spaces (e.g. the line with two origins) allow sequences to converge to more than one point — pathological for analysis.

---

## Normal Spaces

$T_4$ spaces are exactly those where [[13.2.4 Urysohn lemma]] applies: disjoint closed sets can be separated not just by open sets, but by a *continuous function* $f : X \to [0,1]$ with $f = 0$ on one set and $f = 1$ on the other.

Every metric space and every compact Hausdorff space is normal.

---

## Common Mistakes

> [!warning] $T_1$ does not imply Hausdorff
> The cofinite topology on an infinite set is $T_1$ (finite sets are closed) but not $T_2$ — any two nonempty open sets must intersect.

## Practice Problems

1. Show that every metric space is Hausdorff.
2. Prove that in a $T_1$ space, every finite subset is closed.
3. Show that the cofinite topology on an infinite set is $T_1$ but not $T_2$.
4. Prove that compact subsets of Hausdorff spaces are closed.
5. Show that a $T_4$ (normal) space that is also $T_1$ is automatically $T_3$.

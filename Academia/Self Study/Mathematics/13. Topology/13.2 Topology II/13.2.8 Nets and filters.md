## Why Sequences Aren't Enough

In a general topological space (not first-countable), sequences fail to fully describe the topology — a point can be in the closure of a set with no sequence from that set converging to it. **Nets** and **filters** fix this.

---

## Nets

A **net** is a function $x : D \to X$ indexed by a **directed set** $D$ (a preordered set where any two elements have a common upper bound), generalizing sequences (indexed by $\mathbb{N}$).

$$x_d \to x \iff \text{for every neighborhood } U \text{ of } x, \ x_d \in U \text{ eventually}$$

> [!example] Why directed sets generalize $\mathbb{N}$
> The collection of finite subsets of an index set, ordered by inclusion, is directed but has no countable cofinal subset — exactly the situation where sequences fail but nets succeed.

---

## Filters

A **filter** on $X$ is a nonempty collection $\mathcal{F}$ of subsets of $X$, closed under supersets and finite intersections, not containing $\emptyset$. A filter **converges** to $x$ if every neighborhood of $x$ belongs to $\mathcal{F}$.

---

## Compactness via Nets/Filters

> [!tip] The payoff
> $X$ is compact iff every net (equivalently, every filter, equivalently every ultrafilter) has a convergent subnet — this reformulation is what makes the proof of Tychonoff's theorem tractable.

---

## Practice Problems

1. Show that every sequence is a net, with $D = \mathbb{N}$ under the usual order.
2. Construct a net that converges to a limit point not reachable by any sequence, in a suitable non-first-countable space.
3. Verify that the neighborhood filter of a point $x$ converges to $x$.
4. Explain how ultrafilter convergence is used to prove Tychonoff's theorem.

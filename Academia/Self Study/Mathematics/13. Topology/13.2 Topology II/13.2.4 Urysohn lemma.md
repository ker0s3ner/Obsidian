## Statement

**Urysohn's Lemma**: Let $X$ be a normal space ($T_4$), and let $A, B \subseteq X$ be disjoint closed sets. Then there exists a continuous function:

$$f : X \to [0,1], \qquad f(A) = \{0\}, \quad f(B) = \{1\}$$

This upgrades a purely topological separation (disjoint open sets) into an *analytic* one (a real-valued function).

---

## Why It Matters

> [!tip] Normal spaces have "enough" functions
> Urysohn's Lemma guarantees normal spaces have a rich supply of continuous real-valued functions — this is what makes normality useful for embedding spaces into $\mathbb{R}^n$ or Hilbert cubes.

It is the key technical tool behind two major results:

- **Tietze Extension Theorem**: a continuous function on a closed subset of a normal space extends continuously to the whole space.
- **Urysohn Metrization Theorem** (see [[13.2.5 Metrization theorems]]): second-countable regular spaces are metrizable, proved by embedding into a countable product of $[0,1]$'s built from Urysohn functions.

---

## Proof Idea

Construct $f$ as a limit of a nested family of open sets indexed by rationals in $[0,1]$, using normality repeatedly to interpolate a new open set strictly between any two already constructed (a "dyadic" refinement), then define:

$$f(x) = \inf\{q \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1] : x \in U_q\}$$

## Practice Problems

1. Show that every metric space satisfies the hypothesis (normality) needed for Urysohn's Lemma.
2. Explain intuitively why normality alone (without countability) is not enough to metrize a space.
3. State the Tietze Extension Theorem and explain how it follows from Urysohn's Lemma.
4. Give an example of disjoint closed sets in $\mathbb{R}^2$ and describe (informally) a Urysohn function separating them.

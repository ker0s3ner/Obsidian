## Statement

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space and $M \subseteq H$ a **closed** subspace. Every $x \in H$ decomposes uniquely as:

$$x = m + m^\perp, \qquad m \in M,\ m^\perp \in M^\perp$$

where $M^\perp = \{y \in H : \langle y, m\rangle = 0 \ \forall m \in M\}$. Equivalently, $H = M \oplus M^\perp$.

The point $m$ is the unique **closest point** to $x$ in $M$: $\|x - m\| = \inf_{v \in M} \|x - v\|$.

---

## Why Closedness Is Needed

The proof minimizes $\|x-v\|$ over $v \in M$ using strict convexity of the Hilbert norm and completeness of $M$ (as a closed subspace of a complete space, $M$ is itself complete). If $M$ isn't closed, the infimum may not be attained.

> [!tip] This is where Hilbert space geometry beats general Banach spaces
> In a general Banach space, best approximation to a closed convex set may fail to exist or fail to be unique. Hilbert space guarantees both, because the norm is strictly convex (from the parallelogram law).

---

## Riesz Representation Consequence

The projection theorem is the key step in proving every continuous linear functional $\varphi$ on $H$ has the form $\varphi(x) = \langle x, y\rangle$ for a unique $y \in H$ — see [[12.4.5 Dual spaces]].

## Practice Problems

1. Find the orthogonal projection of $(1,2,3)$ onto the plane $x+y+z=0$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
2. Prove $M^{\perp\perp} = \overline{M}$ (closure of $M$) for any subspace $M$.
3. Show that the closest-point map $x \mapsto m$ is linear.
4. Give an example in a non-Hilbert Banach space where a closed convex set has no closest point.

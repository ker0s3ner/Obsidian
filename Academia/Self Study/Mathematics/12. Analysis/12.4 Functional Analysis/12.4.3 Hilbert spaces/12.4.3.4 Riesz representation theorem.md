## Statement

For every bounded linear functional $\varphi \in H^*$ on a Hilbert space $H$, there exists a **unique** $y \in H$ such that:

$$\varphi(x) = \langle x, y\rangle \quad \text{for all } x \in H, \qquad \|\varphi\| = \|y\|$$

The map $y \mapsto \langle \cdot, y\rangle$ is a conjugate-linear isometric bijection $H \to H^*$ — a Hilbert space is **self-dual**.

---

## Proof Sketch

If $\varphi = 0$, take $y = 0$. Otherwise $\ker\varphi$ is a proper closed subspace, so by the [[12.4.3.3 Projection theorem]], $(\ker\varphi)^\perp$ is one-dimensional, spanned by some $z$; set $y = \frac{\overline{\varphi(z)}}{\|z\|^2} z$.

---

## Significance

Riesz representation is what makes Hilbert space theory so much cleaner than general Banach space theory: dual elements are literally vectors of the same space, with the same norm, via a concrete inner product formula — rather than an abstract space $H^*$ as in the general [[12.4.5 Dual spaces]] picture.

> [!example] Application
> In PDE theory, weak solutions to elliptic equations are constructed by applying Riesz representation to a bounded linear functional built from the equation's right-hand side — this is the basis of the **Lax–Milgram theorem**.

## Practice Problems

1. Verify $\|\varphi\| = \|y\|$ in the proof sketch.
2. Show the representation map $y \mapsto \langle\cdot,y\rangle$ is conjugate-linear, not linear, over $\mathbb{C}$.
3. Explain why Riesz representation fails for general Banach spaces (e.g. $\ell^1$, whose dual is $\ell^\infty$, not $\ell^1$).

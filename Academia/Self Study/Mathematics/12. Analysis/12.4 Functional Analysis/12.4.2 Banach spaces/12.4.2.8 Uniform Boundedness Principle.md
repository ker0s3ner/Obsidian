## Statement

Let $X$ be a Banach space, $Y$ a normed space, and $\{T_i\}_{i \in I} \subseteq \mathcal{B}(X,Y)$ a family of bounded operators. If the family is **pointwise bounded**,

$$\sup_{i \in I} \|T_i x\| < \infty \quad \text{for every } x \in X$$

then it is **uniformly bounded**:

$$\sup_{i \in I} \|T_i\| < \infty$$

Also known as the **Banach–Steinhaus theorem**.

---

## Why Completeness of $X$ Is Essential

The proof applies the Baire Category Theorem to the sets $E_n = \{x : \sup_i \|T_ix\| \leq n\}$, which requires $X$ complete for Baire to apply. Without completeness, pointwise boundedness need not imply uniform boundedness.

---

## Applications

- **Fourier series**: proving there exist continuous functions whose Fourier series diverges at a point, by showing the partial-sum operators are *not* uniformly bounded
- Weak convergence: a weakly convergent sequence in a Banach space is automatically norm-bounded

> [!example] Contrapositive strategy
> To show a family of operators is **unbounded** in norm, it suffices to exhibit even one point $x$ where $\sup_i \|T_i x\| = \infty$ — no, wait: to show the family is norm-unbounded, show it is NOT pointwise bounded, i.e. find some $x$ where the values blow up. This is the standard trick for proving divergence phenomena exist.

## Practice Problems

1. State the contrapositive of the theorem and explain how it's used to prove existence of "bad" objects (e.g. divergent Fourier series).
2. Show that pointwise boundedness on a dense subset plus uniform boundedness on that subset implies pointwise boundedness everywhere.
3. Give an example of a family of linear maps on an incomplete space that is pointwise bounded but not uniformly bounded.

## Definition

A normed space $X$ is a **Banach space** if it is **complete**: every Cauchy sequence converges in $X$.

$$(x_n) \text{ Cauchy} \implies \exists\, x \in X : x_n \to x$$

---

## Cauchy vs Convergent

Every convergent sequence is Cauchy in any metric space; completeness is the converse. Incompleteness means Cauchy sequences can "aim at" a limit that doesn't exist in the space.

**Example — $C[0,1]$ with $\|\cdot\|_1$ is NOT complete.** Take $f_n$ approximating the step function $\chi_{[0,1/2]}$ more sharply as $n\to\infty$: $(f_n)$ is Cauchy in $\|\cdot\|_1$, but the limit is discontinuous, so it isn't in $C[0,1]$.

> [!warning] Completeness depends on the norm
> $C[0,1]$ **is** complete under $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ but **not** under $\|\cdot\|_1$. Completeness is a property of the pair $(X, \|\cdot\|)$, not of the underlying vector space alone.

---

## Absolute Convergence Criterion

$X$ is complete if and only if every **absolutely convergent** series converges:

$$\sum_n \|x_n\| < \infty \implies \sum_n x_n \text{ converges in } X$$

This is often the easiest way to prove a space is Banach.

## Practice Problems

1. Show $\mathbb{Q}$ is not complete under the usual absolute-value norm.
2. Prove that a closed subspace of a Banach space is itself Banach.
3. Use the absolute convergence criterion to show $\ell^1$ is complete.
4. Exhibit a Cauchy sequence in $C[0,1]$ (with $\|\cdot\|_1$) that has no limit in $C[0,1]$.

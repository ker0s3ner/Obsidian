## Three Notions of Operator Convergence

For $T_n, T \in \mathcal{B}(X,Y)$:

| Topology | Convergence condition |
|----------|------------------------|
| **Uniform** (norm) | $\|T_n - T\| \to 0$ |
| **Strong** | $T_n x \to Tx$ for every $x \in X$ |
| **Weak** | $\varphi(T_nx) \to \varphi(Tx)$ for every $x \in X$, $\varphi \in Y^*$ |

Uniform convergence implies strong, and strong implies weak — but neither reverse implication holds in general.

---

## Example Distinguishing All Three

On $\ell^2$, let $T_n$ be the shift $T_n(x_1,x_2,\ldots) = (0,\ldots,0,x_1,x_2,\ldots)$ ($n$ zeros). Then $T_n \to 0$ weakly (since $\langle T_nx, y\rangle \to 0$ by Cauchy–Schwarz tail estimates) but $\|T_nx\| = \|x\|$ for all $n$, so $T_n \not\to 0$ strongly, and certainly not uniformly.

> [!tip] Why this matters
> Many "convergence" statements in operator theory (e.g. in quantum mechanics, resolvent convergence) implicitly specify *which* topology — confusing strong and weak convergence is a common source of error.

## Practice Problems

1. Verify the shift-operator example: show $T_n \rightharpoonup 0$ weakly but not strongly.
2. Prove uniform convergence implies strong convergence implies weak convergence.
3. Give an example of a sequence converging strongly but not uniformly.

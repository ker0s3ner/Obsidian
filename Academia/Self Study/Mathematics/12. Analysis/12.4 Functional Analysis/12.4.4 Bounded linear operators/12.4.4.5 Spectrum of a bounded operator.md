## Definitions

For $T \in \mathcal{B}(X)$ on a complex Banach space, the **spectrum** is:

$$\sigma(T) = \{\lambda \in \mathbb{C} : T - \lambda I \text{ is not invertible in } \mathcal{B}(X)\}$$

Its complement, the **resolvent set** $\rho(T) = \mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(T)$, consists of $\lambda$ for which $(T-\lambda I)^{-1}$ exists and is bounded.

| Part of spectrum | Failure mode of $T - \lambda I$ |
|-------------------|----------------------------------|
| Point spectrum | Not injective — $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue |
| Continuous spectrum | Injective, dense range, unbounded inverse |
| Residual spectrum | Injective, non-dense range |

---

## Basic Facts

- $\sigma(T)$ is a **nonempty**, **compact** subset of $\mathbb{C}$, contained in the disk $|\lambda| \leq \|T\|$
- The **spectral radius** $r(T) = \sup_{\lambda \in \sigma(T)} |\lambda|$ satisfies $r(T) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \|T^n\|^{1/n} \leq \|T\|$

> [!note] Contrast with finite dimensions
> In finite dimensions, the spectrum is exactly the set of eigenvalues. In infinite dimensions, $T$ can fail to be invertible **without** having an eigenvalue — this is the continuous/residual spectrum, which has no analogue for matrices.

## Practice Problems

1. Compute the spectrum of the multiplication operator $(Tf)(x) = xf(x)$ on $L^2[0,1]$, and show it has no eigenvalues.
2. Show that $\sigma(T) \subseteq \{|\lambda| \leq \|T\|\}$ using a Neumann series argument.
3. Verify the spectral radius formula for a diagonal operator on $\ell^2$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_n \to 0$.

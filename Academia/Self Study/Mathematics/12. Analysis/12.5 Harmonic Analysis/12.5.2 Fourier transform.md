## Definition

For $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$:

$$\hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) e^{-2\pi i x \xi}\, dx$$

This is the non-compact analogue of Fourier series: instead of decomposing into characters of $\mathbb{T}$ (indexed by $\mathbb{Z}$), it decomposes into characters of $\mathbb{R}$ (indexed by $\mathbb{R}$ itself).

---

## Core Properties

| Property | Statement |
|----------|-----------|
| Linearity | $\widehat{af+bg} = a\hat f + b\hat g$ |
| Differentiation | $\widehat{f'}(\xi) = 2\pi i \xi \hat f(\xi)$ |
| Convolution | $\widehat{f * g} = \hat f \cdot \hat g$ |
| Riemann–Lebesgue | $\hat f(\xi) \to 0$ as $|\xi| \to \infty$ |

> [!example] Uncertainty principle
> The Fourier transform trades localization: a function concentrated in $x$ has a spread-out transform, and vice versa. Quantitatively, $\left(\int x^2|f|^2\right)\left(\int \xi^2|\hat f|^2\right) \geq \frac{1}{16\pi^2}\left(\int |f|^2\right)^2$.

---

## Extension to $L^2$ — Plancherel's Theorem

The Fourier transform extends from $L^1 \cap L^2$ to a unitary isomorphism of $L^2(\mathbb{R})$: $\|\hat f\|_2 = \|f\|_2$. This makes the abstract [[12.4.3.4 Riesz representation theorem|self-duality of Hilbert space]] concrete — $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ is unitarily identified with itself via $\hat{\ }$.

## Practice Problems

1. Compute the Fourier transform of $f(x) = e^{-\pi x^2}$ and verify it's its own transform.
2. Prove the differentiation property $\widehat{f'} = 2\pi i \xi \hat f$ using integration by parts.
3. Use Plancherel's theorem to compute $\int_{-\infty}^\infty \left(\frac{\sin \pi x}{\pi x}\right)^2 dx$.

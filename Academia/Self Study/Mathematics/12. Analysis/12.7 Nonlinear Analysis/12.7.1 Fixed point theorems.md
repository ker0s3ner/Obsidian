## Beyond Contractions

The Banach fixed point theorem ([[12.4 Functional Analysis/12.4.2 Banach spaces/12.4.2.3 Contraction Mapping]]) needs a strict contraction. Nonlinear analysis develops fixed point theorems that need only **continuity** and **compactness/convexity**, at the cost of losing uniqueness and explicit convergence rates.

---

## Brouwer's Fixed Point Theorem

Every continuous map $f: \bar B^n \to \bar B^n$ (closed unit ball in $\mathbb{R}^n$) has a fixed point. Purely topological — no algebraic structure like contraction is needed, but existence only, no uniqueness.

## Schauder's Fixed Point Theorem

The infinite-dimensional generalization: if $K$ is a nonempty compact convex subset of a Banach space and $f: K \to K$ is continuous, $f$ has a fixed point.

> [!example] Why compactness of $K$ matters here
> Unlike Banach's contraction theorem, Schauder's theorem needs no contraction property, but it needs $K$ compact — since closed balls in infinite-dimensional spaces are never compact ([[12.4 Functional Analysis/12.4.1 Normed spaces/12.4.1.3 Finite-dimensional normed spaces]]), applications typically restrict to a compact set built from equicontinuity (Arzelà–Ascoli).

---

## Applications

Existence of solutions to nonlinear ODEs/PDEs (Peano existence theorem, nonlinear elliptic boundary value problems) where uniqueness may genuinely fail.

## Practice Problems

1. Show Brouwer's theorem fails on the open ball $B^n$ (find a fixed-point-free continuous self-map).
2. Explain why Schauder's theorem needs compactness where Banach's needs a contraction instead.
3. Sketch how Schauder's theorem gives existence (without uniqueness) for the Peano existence theorem in ODEs.

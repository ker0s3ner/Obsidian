## Why Fourier Transform Solves Linear PDEs

The Fourier transform converts differentiation into multiplication:

$$\widehat{\partial_x^k f}(\xi) = (2\pi i \xi)^k \hat f(\xi)$$

so a **constant-coefficient** linear PDE becomes an algebraic (or ODE-in-$t$) equation in Fourier space.

---

## Example: The Heat Equation

$$u_t = u_{xx}, \qquad u(x,0) = f(x)$$

Taking the Fourier transform in $x$: $\hat u_t(\xi,t) = -4\pi^2\xi^2\hat u(\xi,t)$, an ODE in $t$ with solution:

$$\hat u(\xi,t) = \hat f(\xi) e^{-4\pi^2\xi^2 t} \implies u(x,t) = f * G_t, \quad G_t(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}e^{-x^2/4t}$$

recovering the heat kernel of [[12.5 Harmonic Analysis/12.5.5 Convolution and approximate identities]].

---

## Example: The Wave Equation

$$u_{tt} = u_{xx} \implies \hat u_{tt} = -4\pi^2\xi^2 \hat u \implies \hat u(\xi,t) = \hat f(\xi)\cos(2\pi\xi t) + \ldots$$

giving d'Alembert's solution after inverse-transforming.

> [!tip] General strategy
> Fourier transform in the spatial variable(s), solve the resulting ODE in $t$ for each frequency $\xi$, then inverse transform. This works whenever the coefficients don't depend on $x$.

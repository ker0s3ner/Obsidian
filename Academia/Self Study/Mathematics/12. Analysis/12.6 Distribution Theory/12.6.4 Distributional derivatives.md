## Definition

The derivative of a distribution $T$ is defined by shifting the derivative onto the (smooth) test function:

$$\langle T', \phi\rangle := -\langle T, \phi'\rangle$$

— the distributional analogue of integration by parts, with the boundary term dropped since $\phi$ has compact support. Every distribution is infinitely differentiable, with $\langle T^{(k)},\phi\rangle = (-1)^k\langle T, \phi^{(k)}\rangle$.

---

## Example: The Heaviside Function

$H(x) = \mathbf{1}_{x\geq0}$. Its distributional derivative is:

$$H' = \delta_0$$

**Verification**: $\langle H',\phi\rangle = -\langle H,\phi'\rangle = -\int_0^\infty \phi'(x)\,dx = \phi(0) = \langle \delta_0,\phi\rangle$.

> [!example] Consequence for physics
> This is why "the derivative of a step is an impulse" is rigorously true — distribution theory makes precise what physicists mean informally.

---

## Consistency with Classical Derivatives

If $f$ is classically differentiable with derivative $f'$, the distributional derivative of $T_f$ agrees with $T_{f'}$ — distributional differentiation extends, rather than replaces, the classical notion.

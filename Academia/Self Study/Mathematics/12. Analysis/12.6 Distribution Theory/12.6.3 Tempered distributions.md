## The Schwartz Space

$\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^n)$: smooth functions all of whose derivatives decay faster than any polynomial. It's larger than $C_c^\infty$ (includes e.g. Gaussians) but small enough that the Fourier transform maps $\mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{S}$ bijectively.

## Tempered Distributions

$\mathcal{S}'(\mathbb{R}^n)$: continuous linear functionals on $\mathcal{S}$. Every polynomially-bounded function, every $L^p$ function, and $\delta_a$ all define tempered distributions — but objects growing faster than any polynomial (e.g. $e^{x^2}$) do not.

---

## Fourier Transform on $\mathcal{S}'$

Since the Fourier transform preserves $\mathcal{S}$, it extends by duality to **all** of $\mathcal{S}'$:

$$\langle \hat T, \phi\rangle := \langle T, \hat\phi\rangle$$

giving every tempered distribution a Fourier transform, including $\delta$ (whose transform is the constant function $1$) and constants (whose transform is $\delta$, up to normalization).

> [!tip] Why this matters
> This is the rigorous foundation for using Fourier transforms of things like the constant function $1$ or the Dirac comb in signal processing — objects with no transform in the classical $L^1$ sense.

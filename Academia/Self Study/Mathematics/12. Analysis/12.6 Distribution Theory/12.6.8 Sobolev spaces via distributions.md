## Definition

The **Sobolev space** $H^k(\Omega)$ ($k$ a non-negative integer) consists of $L^2(\Omega)$ functions all of whose distributional derivatives up to order $k$ are also in $L^2(\Omega)$:

$$\|u\|_{H^k}^2 = \sum_{|\alpha|\leq k} \int_\Omega |\partial^\alpha u|^2 \, dx < \infty$$

Distribution theory is what makes this definition rigorous — $\partial^\alpha u$ need only exist as a distribution and happen to be represented by an $L^2$ function.

---

## Why Sobolev Spaces Need Distributions

**Example**: $u(x) = |x|$ on $(-1,1)$ has classical derivative $u'(x) = \text{sgn}(x)$ (undefined at $0$, but that's a single point, measure zero) — this **is** the distributional derivative, and it's in $L^2$, so $u \in H^1(-1,1)$ even though $u$ isn't classically differentiable everywhere.

---

## Weak Solutions to PDEs

Sobolev spaces are the natural home for **weak solutions**: $u \in H^1$ solves an elliptic PDE weakly if it satisfies the equation tested against every $\phi \in \mathcal{D}$, without requiring $u$ to be twice classically differentiable. This is the standard modern framework for existence theory in [[16. Specialized Topics/PDEs]].

> [!tip] Why this is a Hilbert space
> $H^k$ is a Hilbert space (complete inner product space, [[12.4.3.1 Inner product spaces]]) — this is exactly what allows Riesz representation / Lax–Milgram arguments to produce weak solutions.

## Statement

Given any two ordered sets of $n+2$ points in "general position" (no $n+1$ of them lying on a hyperplane) in $\mathbb{P}^n$, there is a **unique** projective transformation taking one ordered set to the other.

**In $\mathbb{P}^1$**: any 3 distinct points can be mapped to any other 3 distinct points by a unique homography.

**In $\mathbb{P}^2$**: any 4 points in general position (no 3 collinear) can be mapped to any other such 4 points uniquely.

---

## Why This Matters

This theorem is the reason cross-ratio ([[19.2.2 Cross-ratio]]) is the *only* projective invariant of 4 collinear points — once 3 points are fixed by the map, the transformation is completely determined, so the position of a 4th point relative to the first 3 (its cross-ratio) is the sole remaining piece of information.

> [!tip] Practical use
> This theorem underlies "four-point" homography estimation in computer vision: four matched points between two images (in general position) pin down the unique homography relating the views.

## Practice Problems

1. Find the unique Möbius transformation sending $0,1,\infty \mapsto 1,\infty,0$.
2. Explain why 3 points are enough to fix a projective map of $\mathbb{P}^1$ but not $\mathbb{P}^2$.
3. Show that if two homographies agree on $n+2$ points in general position, they are equal.
4. Relate this theorem to why 4-point correspondences are used in image rectification.

## Great Circles as Geodesics

On a sphere of radius $R$, the shortest paths ("lines") are arcs of **great circles** — circles formed by intersecting the sphere with a plane through its center.

---

## Spherical Law of Cosines

For a spherical triangle with sides $a,b,c$ (arc lengths as angles, i.e. divided by $R$) and opposite angles $A,B,C$:

$$\cos c = \cos a \cos b + \sin a \sin b \cos C$$

Compare to the hyperbolic law of cosines in [[19.1.4 Hyperbolic trigonometry]] — the same formula with $\cosh, \sinh \to \cos, \sin$.

---

## Spherical Excess

The area of a spherical triangle of angles $A, B, C$ on a unit sphere:

$$\text{Area} = A + B + C - \pi$$

matching the elliptic geometry result of [[19.1.2 Elliptic geometry]] (spherical geometry is the double cover of elliptic geometry).

> [!warning] Sphere vs. elliptic plane
> On the sphere itself, two great circles meet at **two** antipodal points, not one — true elliptic geometry requires identifying antipodal points first.

## Practice Problems

1. Compute the area of a spherical triangle with all angles $\pi/2$ (an octant of the sphere).
2. Derive the spherical Pythagorean theorem from the law of cosines.
3. Explain why the sum of angles in any spherical triangle always exceeds $\pi$.
4. Show that great circles are the geodesics of the sphere using variational reasoning.

## Local Gauss–Bonnet

For a geodesic triangle $T$ on a surface with Gaussian curvature $K$ and interior angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$:

$$\iint_T K \, dA = \alpha + \beta + \gamma - \pi$$

This single formula explains the angle-sum behavior in all three constant-curvature geometries: negative $K$ gives angle-sum deficit, positive $K$ gives excess, and $K = 0$ recovers Euclid's $\pi$.

---

## Global Gauss–Bonnet

For a closed orientable surface $S$:

$$\iint_S K \, dA = 2\pi \chi(S)$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $\chi(S)$ | Euler characteristic, $\chi = 2 - 2g$ for genus $g$ |

**Example — the sphere** ($g=0$, $\chi = 2$): total curvature is $4\pi$, matching $K=1$ over area $4\pi$.

> [!example] Why this is remarkable
> The right-hand side depends only on the **topology** of $S$ (its genus), while the left-hand side is a purely **geometric** quantity (curvature integrated over area). Bending a surface without tearing it cannot change total curvature.

## Practice Problems

1. Verify the global formula for a torus ($g=1$, $\chi=0$): total curvature must vanish.
2. Use local Gauss–Bonnet to re-derive the hyperbolic triangle area formula.
3. Explain why a surface of genus $\geq 2$ must have regions of negative curvature.
4. What does Gauss–Bonnet imply about a flat ($K=0$) closed surface?

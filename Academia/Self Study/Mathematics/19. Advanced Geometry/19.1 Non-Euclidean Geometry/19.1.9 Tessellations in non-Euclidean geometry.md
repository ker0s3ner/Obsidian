## Regular Tilings and the Angle Condition

A regular tiling by $p$-gons meeting $q$ at each vertex exists when the interior angle of the $p$-gon, times $q$, relates to $2\pi$:

$$\frac{(p-2)\pi}{p} \cdot q \; \begin{cases} = 2\pi & \text{Euclidean} \\ < 2\pi & \text{spherical} \\ > 2\pi & \text{hyperbolic} \end{cases}$$

Equivalently, using $\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q}$ compared to $\frac{1}{2}$:

| $\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}$ | Geometry | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| $> \frac12$ | Spherical | Platonic solids ($\{3,3\}, \{4,3\}, \{3,4\}, \{5,3\}, \{3,5\}$) |
| $= \frac12$ | Euclidean | $\{3,6\}, \{4,4\}, \{6,3\}$ |
| $< \frac12$ | Hyperbolic | $\{7,3\}, \{3,7\}$, and infinitely many more |

---

## Infinitude of Hyperbolic Tilings

Unlike the finite list of Euclidean and spherical regular tilings, **every** pair $(p,q)$ with $\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q} < \frac12$ gives a valid hyperbolic tiling — infinitely many, since triangles in hyperbolic space can have arbitrarily small angle sums.

> [!example] Escher's Circle Limit series
> M.C. Escher's *Circle Limit* woodcuts are hyperbolic tilings drawn in the Poincaré disk model — tiles that look like they shrink toward the boundary are, intrinsically, all congruent.

## Practice Problems

1. Verify that $\{7,3\}$ satisfies the hyperbolic tiling condition.
2. List all 5 Platonic solids as $\{p,q\}$ pairs and confirm each satisfies the spherical condition.
3. Explain why only three regular tilings exist in the Euclidean plane.
4. Why are there infinitely many regular hyperbolic tilings but only finitely many spherical ones?

## Definition

A submanifold $L \subset M$ of the maximal possible dimension $n$ (in a $(2n+1)$-manifold with contact structure $\xi$) is **Legendrian** if it is everywhere tangent to $\xi$:

$$T_pL \subset \xi_p \quad \text{for all } p \in L$$

This is the largest dimension an integral submanifold of $\xi$ can have, by the non-integrability condition in [[19.3.1 Contact structures]].

---

## Example on $\mathbb{R}^3$

With $\alpha = dz - y\,dx$, a curve $\gamma(t) = (x(t), y(t), z(t))$ is Legendrian iff:

$$z'(t) = y(t)\,x'(t)$$

**Front projection**: projecting a Legendrian knot to the $(x,z)$-plane recovers $y = dz/dx$, so the curve is determined entirely by its front, with cusps where the curve is vertical.

> [!tip] Legendrian vs. Lagrangian
> Legendrian submanifolds in contact geometry play the role Lagrangian submanifolds play in symplectic geometry ([[4. Geometry/4.12 Symplectic Geometry]]) — both are the maximal isotropic submanifolds for their respective structure.

## Practice Problems

1. Verify that $\gamma(t) = (t, t^2, \frac{2}{3}t^3)$ is Legendrian for $\alpha = dz - y\,dx$.
2. Sketch the front projection of a simple Legendrian curve and locate its cusps.
3. Explain why Legendrian submanifolds have exactly half the ambient manifold's "free" dimension.
4. Compare the isotropic condition for Legendrian vs. Lagrangian submanifolds.

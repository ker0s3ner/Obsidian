## Statement

For every $r$, there exists $S(r)$ such that any $r$-coloring of $\{1,\ldots,S(r)\}$ contains a monochromatic solution to $x+y=z$.

---

## Proof via Ramsey's Theorem

Given an $r$-coloring $c$ of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, color the edges of $K_n$ (vertices $1,\ldots,n$) by $c(|i-j|)$. A monochromatic triangle $\{i,j,k\}$ with $i<j<k$ gives $x=j-i$, $y=k-j$, $z=k-i$, all the same color, satisfying $x+y=z$. Since Ramsey numbers $R_r(3)$ are finite, so is $S(r)$.

> [!note] Schur numbers grow fast, but slower than $R_r(3)$
> Known Schur numbers: $S(1)=1$, $S(2)=4$, $S(3)=13$, $S(4)=44$ (the last found by SAT solvers in 2017) — this reduction gives $S(r) \le R_r(3) - 1$, though the true values are typically much smaller than that bound.

## Practice Problems

1. Verify $S(2)=4$ by exhibiting a 2-coloring of $\{1,2,3\}$ with no monochromatic solution to $x+y=z$.
2. Reconstruct the reduction from Schur's theorem to Ramsey's theorem in detail.
3. Explain why the Ramsey-based bound on $S(r)$ is far from tight.
4. Look up how the Schur number $S(5)$ was resolved and what techniques were used.

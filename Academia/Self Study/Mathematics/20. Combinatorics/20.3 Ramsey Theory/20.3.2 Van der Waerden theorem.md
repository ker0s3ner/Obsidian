## Statement

For any $k$ (progression length) and $r$ (number of colors), there exists $W(k,r)$ such that any $r$-coloring of $\{1,\ldots,W(k,r)\}$ contains a monochromatic $k$-term arithmetic progression.

---

## Relation to Szemerédi's Theorem

Van der Waerden's theorem (1927) is a **coloring** (Ramsey-type) statement: it only guarantees a monochromatic progression exists in *some* color class, without controlling density. [[20.2.7 Szemerédi's theorem on APs]] is the much stronger **density** statement — any sufficiently dense set (not just "the biggest color class") contains a $k$-AP. Since one color class in an $r$-coloring has density $\geq 1/r$, Szemerédi's theorem implies van der Waerden's, but not conversely as easily.

> [!note] Notoriously fast-growing bounds
> The best known upper bounds on $W(k,r)$ are tower/Ackermann-type in $k$ — proving reasonable bounds is itself a deep combinatorial problem, tackled with the Hales–Jewett theorem's combinatorial cube argument.

## Practice Problems

1. Verify $W(3,2) = 9$ by 2-coloring $\{1,\ldots,8\}$ with no monochromatic 3-AP.
2. Explain why Szemerédi's theorem is strictly stronger than van der Waerden's.
3. Sketch, at a high level, how a compactness argument shows $W(k,r)$ is finite once "arbitrarily long monochromatic APs exist for unbounded intervals" is ruled out.
4. Discuss why the known bounds on $W(k,r)$ grow so much faster than on Ramsey numbers $R(k,k)$.

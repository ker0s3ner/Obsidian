## Generalizing Schur's Theorem

Rado's theorem characterizes exactly which linear equations $c_1x_1 + \cdots + c_kx_k = 0$ are **partition regular** — guaranteed to have a monochromatic solution under any finite coloring of $\mathbb{N}$, for $n$ large enough.

---

## The Rado Condition (Columns Condition)

An equation $\sum c_i x_i = 0$ is partition regular if and only if some nonempty subset of the coefficients $\{c_i\}$ sums to zero — more precisely, the coefficients can be partitioned into groups where a specific "columns condition" involving partial sums holds.

**Example**: $x+y=z$ (i.e. $x+y-z=0$) is partition regular by Rado's criterion — matching Schur's theorem exactly, since $x+y-z=0$ satisfies the columns condition.

> [!example] Non-example
> The equation $x+y = 3z$ turns out to also be partition regular, while other equations like $x - 2y = 1$ (inhomogeneous) fall outside Rado's framework, which is specifically about homogeneous linear equations.

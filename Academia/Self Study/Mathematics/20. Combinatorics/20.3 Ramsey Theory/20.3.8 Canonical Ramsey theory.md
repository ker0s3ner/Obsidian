## Removing the Finite-Color Restriction

Ordinary Ramsey theory requires finitely many colors. **Canonical Ramsey theory** (Erdős–Rado, 1950) asks what happens with an **arbitrary** (possibly infinite) coloring — no monochromatic structure can be guaranteed, but a weaker "canonical" structure always can.

---

## Canonical Ramsey Theorem

> [!example] Erdős–Rado Canonical Ramsey Theorem
> Any coloring (with arbitrarily many colors) of the pairs from an infinite set contains an infinite subset $S$ on which the coloring is **canonical**: for some subset $I \subseteq \{1,2\}$ of coordinate positions, $c(\{x,y\}) = c(\{x',y'\})$ (for $x<y$, $x'<y'$ in $S$) if and only if $x=x'$ and $y=y'$ agree on the positions in $I$.

There are exactly $2^2 = 4$ canonical types for pairs: constant, depends only on the smaller element, only on the larger, or injective (all different).

## Practice Problems

1. List the 4 canonical coloring types for pairs and give an example coloring realizing each.
2. Explain why an arbitrary (infinite-color) coloring cannot guarantee full monochromaticity.
3. Contrast canonical Ramsey theory with ordinary (finite-color) infinite Ramsey theory.
4. Research how canonical Ramsey theory generalizes to $k$-tuples instead of pairs.

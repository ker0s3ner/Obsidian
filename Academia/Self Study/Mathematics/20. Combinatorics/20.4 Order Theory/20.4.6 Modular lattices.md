## Definition

A lattice is **modular** if for all $x \leq z$:

$$x \vee (y \wedge z) = (x \vee y) \wedge z$$

Every distributive lattice is modular (the modular law is a weaker form of distributivity restricted to the case $x \leq z$), but the converse fails.

---

## Forbidden Substructure

A lattice is modular if and only if it contains no sublattice isomorphic to the pentagon $N_5$ — weaker than the distributive obstruction (which also forbids $M_3$). So $M_3$ (the diamond, i.e. the lattice of subspaces of a 2-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{F}_2$) is modular but **not** distributive.

---

## Key Example: Subspace Lattices

The lattice of subspaces of a vector space, ordered by inclusion with join = sum and meet = intersection, is always modular — this reflects the dimension formula:

$$\dim(U+V) + \dim(U\cap V) = \dim U + \dim V$$

but generally fails to be distributive once the space has dimension $\geq 2$ over a field with more than 2 elements.

> [!tip] Modularity as a rank condition
> Modular lattices are exactly the natural setting for **matroid rank functions** — the modular law is essentially the submodularity/rank-additivity condition seen in [[20.5 Matroid Theory/20.5.1 Matroid axioms]].

## Practice Problems

1. Verify the modular law for the subspace lattice of $\mathbb{R}^3$ with two specific 1-dimensional subspaces.
2. Show that $M_3$ satisfies the modular law but not the distributive law.
3. Explain the connection between the modular law and the dimension formula for vector spaces.
4. Give an example of a lattice that is neither modular nor distributive.

## Complete Lattices

A lattice is **complete** if every subset (not just finite pairs) has a join and meet — in particular, a global minimum $\bigwedge \emptyset$ and maximum $\bigvee \emptyset$ exist.

**Example**: $(\mathcal{P}(X), \subseteq)$ is complete — arbitrary unions and intersections always exist.

---

## Knaster–Tarski Fixed Point Theorem

> [!example] Knaster–Tarski Theorem
> Every order-preserving function $f: L \to L$ on a complete lattice $L$ has a **fixed point** — in fact, the set of fixed points forms a complete lattice itself, with least fixed point $\bigwedge\{x : f(x) \leq x\}$.

---

## Application: Existence of Semantics

This theorem underlies the existence of least and greatest fixed points used to define recursive definitions rigorously — e.g. the semantics of recursive programs, inductively defined sets, and the Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein theorem's constructive proof.

> [!tip] No topology required
> Unlike the Banach fixed point theorem (which needs a metric and contraction), Knaster–Tarski needs **only order structure** — this is what makes it applicable to purely combinatorial and logical settings.

## Practice Problems

1. Verify that $(\mathcal{P}(X), \subseteq)$ is a complete lattice.
2. Use Knaster–Tarski to prove the Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein theorem's key lemma.
3. Explain why order-preservation (not continuity) is the only hypothesis needed here.
4. Construct an order-preserving map on a complete lattice with more than one fixed point.

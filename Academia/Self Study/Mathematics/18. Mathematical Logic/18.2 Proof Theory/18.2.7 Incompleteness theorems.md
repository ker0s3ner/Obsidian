## Gödel's First Incompleteness Theorem

> [!example] First Incompleteness Theorem
> Any consistent, sufficiently strong, effectively axiomatized theory $T$ (able to formalize basic arithmetic) has a sentence $G$ that is **true but unprovable** in $T$.

**Idea**: $G$ is constructed to (indirectly, via arithmetic coding of syntax) assert "$G$ is not provable in $T$." If $T \vdash G$, then $G$ is false, so $T$ proves a falsehood — assuming consistency, $T \nvdash G$, which is exactly what $G$ says, so $G$ is true.

---

## Second Incompleteness Theorem

> [!example] Second Incompleteness Theorem
> No such theory $T$ can prove its own consistency statement $\text{Con}(T)$ (unless $T$ is inconsistent).

This is why [[18.2.5 Ordinal analysis]]'s consistency proof for Peano Arithmetic must use transfinite induction up to $\varepsilon_0$ — a principle PA itself cannot prove — rather than anything internal to PA.

---

## Scope and Limits

> [!warning] Not every theory is affected
> Incompleteness requires the theory to represent enough arithmetic to encode self-reference (Gödel numbering). Weaker decidable theories, like Presburger arithmetic (see [[18.1.6 Quantifier elimination]]), escape incompleteness entirely because they lack this expressive power.

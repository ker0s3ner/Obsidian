## Definition

A theory $T$ is **$\kappa$-categorical** if it has exactly one model of cardinality $\kappa$, up to isomorphism.

By [[18.1.4 Lowenheim-Skolem theorem]], no theory with an infinite model is categorical in *every* cardinality — but it can be categorical in a specific one.

---

## Vaught's Test

> [!example] Vaught's Test
> If $T$ has no finite models and is $\kappa$-categorical for **some** infinite $\kappa \geq |\mathcal L|$, then $T$ is **complete** (decides every sentence).

**Example**: the theory of dense linear orders without endpoints is $\aleph_0$-categorical (all countable models are isomorphic to $(\mathbb Q, <)$), hence complete.

---

## Morley's Categoricity Theorem

> [!note] Morley's Theorem
> If a countable theory $T$ is categorical in **one** uncountable cardinality, it is categorical in **every** uncountable cardinality.

This deep 1965 result launched modern stability theory: categoricity in one uncountable cardinal is an all-or-nothing structural property of the theory, not an accident of a particular cardinal.

## Practice Problems

1. Show the theory of algebraically closed fields of a fixed characteristic is categorical in every uncountable cardinality (this is a classical example of Morley's theorem in action).
2. Use Vaught's test to argue the theory of dense linear orders without endpoints is complete.
3. Why must $\aleph_0$-categoricity be excluded from Morley's theorem's conclusion (it only claims uncountable cardinalities agree)?

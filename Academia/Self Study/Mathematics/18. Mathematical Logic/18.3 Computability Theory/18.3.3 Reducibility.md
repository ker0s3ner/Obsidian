## Many-One Reducibility

$A$ is **many-one reducible** to $B$ ($A \leq_m B$) if there is a computable function $f$ with:

$$x \in A \iff f(x) \in B$$

If $A \leq_m B$ and $B$ is decidable, so is $A$ — this is the standard technique for proving undecidability by reduction, used throughout [[18.3.2 Decidability]].

---

## Turing Reducibility

$A$ is **Turing reducible** to $B$ ($A \leq_T B$) if $A$ is decidable by a machine with an **oracle** for $B$ — a black box that instantly answers membership queries in $B$. This is a strictly more permissive notion than many-one reducibility.

---

## Degrees of Unsolvability

Turing reducibility induces equivalence classes (**Turing degrees**), measuring relative computational difficulty. The Halting Problem's degree, $\mathbf{0}'$, sits strictly above the decidable languages' degree $\mathbf{0}$.

> [!note] Not all undecidable problems are equally hard
> There exist undecidable sets strictly between decidable sets and the Halting Problem in Turing degree (Friedberg–Muchnik theorem) — reducibility lets computability theory distinguish "how undecidable" a problem is, not just decidable-vs-not.

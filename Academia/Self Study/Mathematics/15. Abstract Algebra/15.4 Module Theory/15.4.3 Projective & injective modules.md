## Projective Modules

$P$ is **projective** if every surjection $M \twoheadrightarrow N$ and homomorphism $P \to N$ lifts to $P \to M$ — equivalently, $P$ is a **direct summand of a free module**.

Every free module is projective; the converse can fail.

---

## Injective Modules

$Q$ is **injective** if every injection $N \hookrightarrow M$ and homomorphism $N \to Q$ extends to $M \to Q$ — the dual notion, "absorbing" maps from the outside.

**Example**: $\mathbb{Q}$ is an injective $\mathbb{Z}$-module; $\mathbb{Z}$ itself is not.

> [!tip] Duality
> Projective and injective are exactly dual under "reversing arrows" — this is the module-theoretic setup that leads to derived functors and Ext groups.

---

## Baer's Criterion

An $R$-module $Q$ is injective iff every homomorphism $I \to Q$ from an ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$ extends to $R \to Q$ — checking injectivity reduces to testing against ideals alone.

## Practice Problems

1. Show every free module is projective.
2. Show $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a projective $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$-module (trivially, as the whole ring), but not projective over $\mathbb{Z}$ unless $n=0$ context is adjusted — discuss.
3. Verify $\mathbb{Q}$ is an injective $\mathbb{Z}$-module.
4. State Baer's criterion and use it to argue divisibility is key for injectivity over $\mathbb{Z}$.
5. Is every projective module free? Discuss with an example (e.g. over $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$).

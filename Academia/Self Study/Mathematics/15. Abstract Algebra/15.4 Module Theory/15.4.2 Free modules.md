## Definition

A **free module** $F$ has a **basis** — a linearly independent generating set — so $F \cong R^{(I)} = \bigoplus_{i\in I} R$ for some index set $I$.

Free modules satisfy the same universal property as free groups: any function from the basis into another module extends uniquely to a module homomorphism.

---

## Not All Modules Are Free

Unlike vector spaces (always free over a field), general modules need not be free.

**Example**: $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is **not free** — any nonzero element $m$ satisfies $2m=0$, so it can't be part of a basis (bases require $rm=0 \implies r=0$).

> [!warning] Torsion blocks freeness
> A module with **torsion** (nonzero $m$ with $rm=0$ for some nonzero $r$) is never free.

---

## Rank

For $R$ a domain, free modules of the same rank (size of basis) are isomorphic, and rank is well-defined — a genuine invariant, generalizing vector space dimension.

## Practice Problems

1. Show $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is not a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module.
2. Verify $R^n$ is a free $R$-module with the standard basis.
3. State and use the universal property of free modules to construct a homomorphism $R^2 \to M$.
4. Show every module is a quotient of some free module.
5. Is $\mathbb{Q}$ a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module?

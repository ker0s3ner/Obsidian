## Statement

For irreducible representations $V, W$ of $G$ over $\mathbb{C}$, and any $G$-equivariant map $\varphi: V \to W$:

1. If $V \not\cong W$: $\varphi = 0$
2. If $V = W$: $\varphi = \lambda \cdot \text{id}$ for some scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$

---

## Proof Idea

$\ker\varphi$ and $\operatorname{im}\varphi$ are subrepresentations; irreducibility forces each to be $0$ or the whole space, giving case (1) directly. For case (2), $\varphi$ has an eigenvalue $\lambda$ (over $\mathbb{C}$), and $\varphi - \lambda\cdot\text{id}$ is then a non-invertible equivariant map on the same irreducible $V$, forcing it to be $0$.

> [!tip] Why $\mathbb{C}$ matters
> Case (2) uses that every operator over $\mathbb{C}$ has an eigenvalue — Schur's lemma can fail in this sharp form over $\mathbb{R}$ or other non-algebraically-closed fields.

---

## Consequence: Orthogonality

Schur's lemma is the structural reason behind the character orthogonality relations — averaging any equivariant map between distinct irreducibles must vanish, which is exactly the computation underlying $\langle \chi_i,\chi_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$.

## Practice Problems

1. Prove that $\ker\varphi$ and $\operatorname{im}\varphi$ are subrepresentations, for equivariant $\varphi$.
2. Show that if $\varphi:V\to V$ is equivariant and $V$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{C}$, then $\varphi$ is a scalar.
3. Give an example over $\mathbb{R}$ where an irreducible representation admits an equivariant map that is not a real scalar multiple of the identity.
4. Use Schur's lemma to justify that abelian groups have all irreducibles 1-dimensional over $\mathbb{C}$.
5. Explain the connection between Schur's lemma and the orthogonality relations.

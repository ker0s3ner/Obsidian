## Definition

For $\varphi: G \to H$, the **kernel** is $\ker\varphi = \{g \in G : \varphi(g) = e_H\}$.

---

## Kernel Is Always Normal

For any $g \in G$ and $k \in \ker\varphi$:

$$\varphi(gkg^{-1}) = \varphi(g)\varphi(k)\varphi(g)^{-1} = \varphi(g) e_H \varphi(g)^{-1} = e_H$$

so $gkg^{-1} \in \ker\varphi$, proving $\ker\varphi \trianglelefteq G$.

---

## Converse: Every Normal Subgroup Is a Kernel

Given $N \trianglelefteq G$, the **quotient map** $\pi: G \to G/N$, $\pi(g) = gN$, is a homomorphism with $\ker\pi = N$. So "normal subgroup" and "kernel of some homomorphism" describe exactly the same objects.

> [!tip] Kernels test injectivity
> $\varphi$ is injective iff $\ker\varphi = \{e\}$ — the single most useful fact for checking whether a homomorphism is an embedding.

## Practice Problems

1. Find $\ker(\text{sgn}: S_n \to \{\pm1\})$.
2. Prove the quotient map $\pi: G \to G/N$ has kernel exactly $N$.
3. Show directly that $\ker\varphi$ is closed under the group operation and inverses.
4. Find a homomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and its kernel.
5. If $\ker\varphi = G$, what can you say about $\varphi$?

## Statement

**Cayley's Theorem**: Every group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group $S_G$ (permutations of the underlying set of $G$). If $|G| = n$, then $G$ embeds in $S_n$.

---

## The Construction

Define $\varphi: G \to S_G$ by $\varphi(g) = \lambda_g$, where $\lambda_g(x) = gx$ (left multiplication by $g$).

- Each $\lambda_g$ is a bijection of $G$ (it has inverse $\lambda_{g^{-1}}$), so $\lambda_g \in S_G$.
- $\varphi$ is a homomorphism: $\lambda_{gh} = \lambda_g \circ \lambda_h$.
- $\varphi$ is injective: if $\lambda_g = \text{id}$, then $ge = e$, so $g = e$.

Hence $\varphi$ embeds $G$ into $S_G \cong S_n$.

> [!tip] Why this matters
> Cayley's theorem shows permutation groups are "universal" — every abstract group can be realized concretely as permutations, which is why $S_n$ and its subgroups are studied so intensively.

---

## Example

For $G = \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z} = \{0, 1, 2\}$, left multiplication by $1$ sends $0\to1, 1\to2, 2\to0$ — the 3-cycle $(0\,1\,2)$. This embeds $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ as $\langle (0\,1\,2) \rangle \leq S_3$.

## Practice Problems

1. Explicitly construct the embedding of $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ into $S_4$ via Cayley's theorem.
2. Show that $\lambda_g \circ \lambda_h = \lambda_{gh}$.
3. Why is the regular representation (left multiplication) always faithful?
4. For $G = S_3$, what is the image of Cayley's embedding inside $S_6$?
5. Explain why Cayley's theorem, while always true, is rarely the most efficient way to represent a group.

## Sum, Intersection, Product

For ideals $I, J \trianglelefteq R$:

| Operation | Definition | Is an ideal? |
|-----------|-----------|----------------|
| $I + J$ | $\{a+b : a\in I, b\in J\}$ | Yes — smallest ideal containing both |
| $I \cap J$ | usual intersection | Yes |
| $IJ$ | finite sums $\sum a_ib_i$, $a_i\in I, b_i \in J$ | Yes |

Always $IJ \subseteq I \cap J$; equality can fail.

---

## Comaximal Ideals

$I, J$ are **comaximal** if $I+J=R$. In that case $IJ = I \cap J$ — this is exactly the hypothesis needed for the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

> [!example] $\mathbb{Z}$ example
> For $I=(4), J=(6)$: $I+J=(2)\neq\mathbb{Z}$, so not comaximal, and indeed $IJ=(24) \neq I\cap J = (12)$.

## Practice Problems

1. Compute $I+J$, $I\cap J$, $IJ$ for $I=(6), J=(10)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$.
2. Prove $IJ \subseteq I \cap J$ always.
3. Show that if $I+J=R$, then $IJ = I\cap J$.
4. Find two ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ that are comaximal.
5. Show $I \cap J$ is always an ideal.

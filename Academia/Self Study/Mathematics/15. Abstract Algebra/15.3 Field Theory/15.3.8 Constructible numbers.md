## Definition

A real number is **constructible** if it can be obtained from $0,1$ using straightedge-and-compass operations (finitely many intersections of lines and circles).

---

## Field-Theoretic Characterization

$\alpha$ is constructible iff $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]$ is a power of $2$, and more precisely iff $\alpha$ lies in a tower of quadratic extensions $\mathbb{Q} = F_0 \subset F_1 \subset \cdots \subset F_k$ with $[F_{i+1}:F_i]=2$.

> [!example] Classical impossibility results
> - **Doubling the cube** ($\sqrt[3]2$): $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]2):\mathbb{Q}]=3$, not a power of 2 — impossible.
> - **Squaring the circle** ($\sqrt\pi$): $\pi$ is transcendental, so $[\mathbb{Q}(\pi):\mathbb{Q}]=\infty$ — impossible.
> - **Trisecting a 60° angle** ($\cos20°$): satisfies a degree-3 minimal polynomial — impossible.

## Practice Problems

1. Show $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is not constructible.
2. Explain why $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]$ being a power of 2 is necessary but not always sufficient for constructibility.
3. Show that a regular $n$-gon is constructible iff $\varphi(n)$ is a power of 2.
4. Why does angle trisection fail for 60° but succeed for some other angles?
5. Show $\sqrt{1+\sqrt2}$ is constructible by exhibiting the tower of quadratic extensions.

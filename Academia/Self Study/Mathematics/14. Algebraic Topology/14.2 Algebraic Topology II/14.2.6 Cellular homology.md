## Definition

For a CW complex $X$, **cellular homology** uses the chain complex $C_n^{CW}(X) = \mathbb{Z}\{n\text{-cells}\}$ with boundary maps given by attaching-map degrees, and computes the same groups as singular homology:

$$H_n^{CW}(X) \cong H_n(X)$$

but from a much smaller, purely combinatorial complex.

---

## Why It's the Practical Tool

> [!tip] Finite computation
> A CW complex with finitely many cells gives a finite chain complex — homology reduces to computing kernels and images of integer matrices, entirely algorithmic.

**Example**: for $T^2$ with one 0-cell, two 1-cells, one 2-cell attached via $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$, all boundary maps vanish, giving directly $H_0 = \mathbb{Z}$, $H_1 = \mathbb{Z}^2$, $H_2 = \mathbb{Z}$.

## Practice Problems

1. Write down the cellular chain complex for $\mathbb{R}P^2$ (one cell in each dimension 0, 1, 2) and compute its homology.
2. Verify the Euler characteristic computed from cellular homology matches $\chi = \sum (-1)^n c_n$.
3. Explain why cellular and singular homology must agree, at least informally.

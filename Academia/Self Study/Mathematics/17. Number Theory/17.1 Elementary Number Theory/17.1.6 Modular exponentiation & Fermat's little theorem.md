## Fermat's Little Theorem

> [!example] Fermat's Little Theorem
> If $p$ is prime and $\gcd(a,p) = 1$:
> $$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$$
> Equivalently, $a^p \equiv a \pmod p$ for **any** integer $a$.

**Example**: $2^{6} = 64 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$ since $7$ is prime and $\gcd(2,7)=1$.

---

## Fast Modular Exponentiation

To compute $a^n \bmod m$ efficiently, use **repeated squaring**:

$$a^n = \begin{cases} (a^{n/2})^2 & n \text{ even} \\ a \cdot a^{n-1} & n \text{ odd}\end{cases}$$

reducing mod $m$ at every step — this runs in $O(\log n)$ multiplications instead of $O(n)$.

---

## Applications

- **Primality testing** (Fermat test): if $a^{n-1} \not\equiv 1 \pmod n$ for some $a$, then $n$ is composite
- **RSA cryptography**: relies on modular exponentiation being fast to compute but hard to invert without the factorization of $n$

> [!warning] Fermat's theorem doesn't certify primality
> Some composite $n$ (**Carmichael numbers**, e.g. $561$) satisfy $a^{n-1}\equiv 1$ for all $a$ coprime to $n$, fooling the naive test.

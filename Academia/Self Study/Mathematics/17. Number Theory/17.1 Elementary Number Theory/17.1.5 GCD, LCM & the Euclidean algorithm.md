## Definitions

$$\gcd(a,b) = \max\{d : d \mid a, \ d \mid b\}, \qquad \text{lcm}(a,b) = \frac{|ab|}{\gcd(a,b)}$$

---

## Euclidean Algorithm

Repeated division: $\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)$, terminating when the remainder is $0$.

**Example — $\gcd(252, 105)$:**

$$252 = 2 \cdot 105 + 42, \quad 105 = 2 \cdot 42 + 21, \quad 42 = 2 \cdot 21 + 0 \implies \gcd = 21$$

---

## Extended Euclidean Algorithm

Finds integers $x, y$ with:

$$ax + by = \gcd(a,b)$$

by tracking coefficients backward through the division steps — this is exactly the identity needed for [[17.1.4 Diophantine equations]] and for computing modular inverses.

> [!tip] Bézout's identity
> $\gcd(a,b)$ is always expressible as an integer combination of $a$ and $b$ — this is what makes modular inverses exist whenever $\gcd(a,n)=1$.

## Practice Problems

1. Compute $\gcd(1071, 462)$ via the Euclidean algorithm.
2. Find integers $x,y$ with $1071x + 462y = \gcd(1071,462)$.
3. Show $\text{lcm}(a,b)\cdot\gcd(a,b) = ab$ for positive integers $a,b$.
4. Use the extended Euclidean algorithm to find the inverse of $7$ mod $26$.

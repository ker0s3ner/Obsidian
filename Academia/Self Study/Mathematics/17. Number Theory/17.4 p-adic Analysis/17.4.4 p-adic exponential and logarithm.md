## p-adic Exponential

$$\exp_p(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}$$

Unlike the real exponential (converges everywhere), this converges only for $|x|_p < p^{-1/(p-1)}$ — because $v_p(n!)$ grows too slowly to tame $x^n/n!$ everywhere.

---

## p-adic Logarithm

$$\log_p(1+x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n-1}x^n}{n}$$

converges for $|x|_p < 1$, and on its region of convergence, $\exp_p$ and $\log_p$ are mutual inverses just as in the real case.

---

## Why Convergence Is Restrictive

> [!warning] Radius of convergence is small
> $v_p(n!) = \sum_i \lfloor n/p^i\rfloor \approx n/(p-1)$, so $|n!|_p \approx p^{-n/(p-1)}$ shrinks fast — making $|x^n/n!|_p$ hard to control except very close to $x=0$. This is why $\exp_p$ has a strictly smaller domain than its real counterpart.

## Practice Problems

1. Determine the radius of convergence of $\exp_p$ for $p=5$.
2. Show $\log_p(1+p)$ converges for any prime $p$.
3. Verify $\exp_p(\log_p(1+x)) = 1+x$ formally by composing the series (to low order).
